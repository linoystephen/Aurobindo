For healthcare professionals

Key Psychiatric Disorders

Quick primary-care algorithms for depression, anxiety and psychosis. Pick a condition, then follow the steps.

⚠️ In every condition: ask directly about suicide and self-harm. Asking does not increase risk.
1

Suspect

Low mood or loss of interest for 2 weeks or more, plus fatigue, sleep or appetite change, poor concentration, guilt or hopelessness.

Also think of it with unexplained physical symptoms: pain, palpitations, headache.

2

Screen

PHQ-2 ≥ 3 is positive, so complete the PHQ-9. Record the total and item 9.

3

Assess and exclude

⚠️ Suicide / self-harm risk
Ask at every visit. Imminent risk: stay with the patient, remove means, arrange urgent specialist care.
Rule out other diagnoses
Past mania or hypomania (bipolar), psychosis, bereavement, anxiety disorder, dementia in older adults.
Medical and substance causes
Thyroid disease, anaemia, B12 deficiency, chronic illness, alcohol or drug use, medicines such as steroids. Test as indicated.
4

Grade severity and manage

Tap the PHQ-9 total score band:

Minimal (0–4)
  • No treatment needed
  • Reassure and advise to return if symptoms persist or worsen
Mild (5–9)
  • Psychoeducation: depression is common and treatable
  • Behavioural activation, regular activity, exercise
  • Sleep routine, reduce alcohol
  • Problem-solving support
  • Review in 2–4 weeks
  • Antidepressants are not routine first-line
Moderate (10–14)
  • Psychological therapy: behavioural activation, CBT, interpersonal therapy or problem-solving
  • and/or an antidepressant (an SSRI such as fluoxetine is a usual first choice)
  • Review 1–2 weeks after starting for side effects and suicidal thoughts
  • Reassess at 4–6 weeks
Moderately severe to severe (15+)
  • Antidepressant plus psychological therapy
  • Seek specialist input early
  • Weekly follow-up at first
  • Check suicide risk at every visit
  • Involve family or carers with consent

💊 Antidepressant practice points

  • Full effect takes about 4–6 weeks. Counsel that benefit is delayed.
  • Extra vigilance for suicidal thoughts in under-25s in the first weeks.
  • Avoid benzodiazepines as treatment for depression. Prefer agents safer than TCAs where overdose is a concern.
  • After remission, continue for several months (commonly 6–12), then taper gradually.

🚩 Refer when

  • Psychotic features or suspected bipolar disorder
  • High or imminent suicide risk
  • No response after an adequate trial (about 6–8 weeks, or two agents)
  • Pregnancy or breastfeeding, adolescents, or complex older-adult cases

1

Suspect

Excessive, hard-to-control worry on most days for months, with restlessness, fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension or poor sleep.

Often presents as physical symptoms: palpitations, headache, chest tightness, stomach upset, dizziness. Ask about panic attacks.

2

Screen

GAD-2 ≥ 3 is positive, so complete the GAD-7. A score of 10 or more suggests probable generalised anxiety disorder and needs clinical assessment.

Also screen for depression with the PHQ-9, as the two often co-occur.

3

Assess and exclude

⚠️ Suicide / self-harm risk
Ask directly. Anxiety with depression, substance use or insomnia raises risk.
Other anxiety-related disorders
Panic disorder, social anxiety, PTSD, OCD, depression, bipolar disorder. Ask about trauma and about compulsions.
Medical, drug and substance causes
Medical: hyperthyroidism, arrhythmia, anaemia, hypoglycaemia, asthma or COPD.
Substances and drugs: caffeine, alcohol or sedative withdrawal, stimulants, cannabis, salbutamol, steroids, excess thyroxine.
4

Grade severity and manage

Tap the GAD-7 total score band:

Minimal (0–4)
  • No treatment needed
  • Reassure and advise to return if worry persists or worsens
Mild (5–9)
  • Explain anxiety and its physical symptoms
  • Cut down caffeine, alcohol and nicotine
  • Regular exercise, sleep routine, slow breathing and relaxation
  • Guided self-help where available
  • Review in 2–4 weeks
Moderate (10–14)
  • CBT or other structured psychological therapy
  • and/or an SSRI (such as sertraline or escitalopram), or an SNRI (such as duloxetine or venlafaxine)
  • Warn that symptoms can briefly worsen early on
  • Review at 2–4 weeks, then judge response over 8–12 weeks
Severe (15+)
  • Psychological therapy plus SSRI or SNRI
  • Seek specialist input early
  • Treat co-existing depression
  • Frequent follow-up; check suicide risk

💊 Medication practice points

  • Avoid benzodiazepines for routine treatment. If used in a crisis, keep to the shortest course (about 2–4 weeks).
  • Start low and go slow with SSRIs and SNRIs. Taper gradually to avoid discontinuation symptoms.
  • If an SSRI or SNRI is not tolerated, pregabalin is an option in some guidelines. Weigh misuse and dependence risk.
  • Continue for about 6–12 months after a good response.

🚩 Refer when

  • No response after two different interventions
  • Severe impairment or high suicide risk
  • Comorbid substance dependence, psychosis or bipolar disorder
  • Suspected PTSD or OCD needing specialist therapy
  • Pregnancy or diagnostic uncertainty

1

Suspect

Hallucinations (often voices), delusions, disorganised speech or behaviour, marked withdrawal, self-neglect, odd or agitated behaviour. Onset is commonly in the late teens to thirties.

2

Assess

There is no brief screening scale. Use a clinical interview and mental state examination. Ask about onset (acute or gradual), duration, substance use, family history and recent stressors.

⚠️ Risk assessment
Suicide and self-harm, harm to others, self-neglect, vulnerability and exploitation. Speak to family or carers if the patient has little insight.
3

Exclude other causes

🚨 Emergencies
Delirium (fluctuating consciousness, acute onset, fever, infection), postpartum psychosis, catatonia, and neuroleptic malignant syndrome (fever, rigidity, altered consciousness) need urgent hospital care.
Substance, drug and mood causes
Cannabis, stimulants, alcohol withdrawal, steroids, levodopa. Mania or severe depression with psychotic features.
Medical and neurological causes
Epilepsy, brain lesion, HIV, neurosyphilis, thyroid disease, B12 deficiency, metabolic disturbance, autoimmune encephalitis. Consider blood count, electrolytes, glucose, kidney, liver and thyroid tests, urine drug screen, HIV and syphilis serology, and brain imaging if there are focal signs or atypical features.
4

Manage

Tap the situation:

Acute or agitated
  • Safety first: calm, low-stimulation space, adequate staff
  • Verbal de-escalation. Do not argue with delusions
  • Treat any reversible cause (intoxication, withdrawal, delirium)
  • If medication is needed, prefer oral: a short-acting benzodiazepine or an antipsychotic, per local protocol
  • Monitor sedation, breathing, blood pressure and ECG where possible
First episode
  • Refer to specialist services where available. Where not, start treatment as in mhGAP
  • Baseline: weight, BMI, waist, blood pressure, glucose, lipids, ECG if risk factors
  • One antipsychotic, low starting dose, titrated, chosen by shared decision
  • Adequate trial: about 4–6 weeks at an effective dose
  • Psychoeducation and family involvement from the start
  • Advise avoiding cannabis and alcohol
Ongoing care
  • Review adherence, side effects (movement disorders, sedation, weight gain), early relapse signs
  • Monitor weight, glucose and lipids at least yearly, and treat physical health
  • Psychosocial support: family, housing, work, rehabilitation
  • Continue treatment at least 1–2 years after a first episode. Never stop abruptly
  • No response to two adequate trials: refer for clozapine assessment

🚩 Refer when

  • Any first episode, where psychiatric services exist
  • Risk to self or others, or unable to care for self
  • Poor response or intolerable side effects
  • Co-existing substance dependence
  • Pregnancy, adolescents or older adults
